Research Dossier: SIH26139
Bottom line: TB detection from chest X-rays is a credible demonstrator for SIH26139, but “segmentation → deep features → dimensionality reduction → QML classifier → explanation” is not itself demonstrably novel. The strongest defensible opportunity is a rigorous evaluation of whether anatomically constrained preprocessing improves the robustness, calibration, low-data behavior, and external generalization of small-qubit QML—not a claim that QML is clinically superior.

The immediate SIH project should be framed as a hybrid clinical-AI research platform, with TB-CXR as the first module. The longer-term paper should focus on a controlled evidence question: when, if ever, does a low-qubit quantum model add measurable value after clinically meaningful feature engineering?

3. Literature position
Established evidence
Classical TB-CXR AI is mature enough to be a serious benchmark domain. Shenzhen has 662 PA CXRs—336 TB and 326 normal—and Montgomery has 138 PA CXRs—58 TB and 80 normal.

TBX11K materially improves the scale of TB-CXR work. It contains 11,200 CXRs, with TB-area bounding-box annotations and categories including healthy, sick-but-non-TB, active TB, latent TB, and uncertain TB.

QML health evidence remains immature. The best systematic evidence concludes that performance differences between QML and classical models in digital health are inconsistent, often poorly benchmarked, and not enough to establish empirical quantum utility.

Low-dimensional preprocessing is standard for near-term QML. QML must encode classical data, and encoding cost is a central bottleneck. Angle encoding requires

Segmentation and localization are not synonymous. TBX11K supports lesion-region bounding boxes, not dense lesion masks as the primary public annotation form. Lung masks are available or can be generated for some classic TB collections, but lesion segmentation requires a separately justified dataset and annotation strategy.

Emerging evidence
QML has been used for medical-image classification, generally via classical feature extraction followed by low-dimensional quantum kernels or variational circuits.

Small circuits, simulators, idealized noise-free evaluations, and weak baseline matching remain common limitations.

A reported TB quanvolutional/QCNN study exists, with 87% validation accuracy, so a generic “QML for TB CXR” claim would not be novel. However, the surfaced source is not sufficient to treat it as strong peer-reviewed evidence.

There is limited high-rigor evidence specifically combining TB CXR, explicit lung segmentation ablation, feature-reduction ablation, matched classical baselines, external dataset testing, calibration, and realistic QML resource reporting.

The paper-level question
A publishable research question is not:

“Can QML detect TB from chest X-rays?”

It is:

“Does lung-field restriction improve the reliability and data efficiency of low-qubit quantum-kernel or variational classifiers based on fixed deep radiographic embeddings, relative to matched classical classifiers, under internal and external TB-CXR evaluation?”

That question can produce a valuable paper even if the answer is “no.”

Strongest defensible gaps
Anatomical-prior ablation for QML: Few high-quality studies appear to test whether restricting representations to segmented lung fields improves QML reliability relative to whole-CXR representations.

External generalization: QML-TB studies with source-separated external validation, patient-level splits, and matched classical controls appear limited.

Low-data learning curves: The claim that QML helps in low-data settings is common, but disease-imaging evidence with repeated subsampling, confidence intervals, and matched feature spaces is insufficient.

Clinical reliability rather than headline accuracy: Calibration, uncertainty-based abstention, failure analysis, and explanation-localization agreement are underreported in QML imaging.

Resource-accounted benchmark: There is a lack of end-to-end comparisons that report preprocessing cost, feature compression, circuit size/depth, shots, simulator/hardware runtime, and matched classical alternatives.

Meaningful evidence
A meaningful result is not “QSVM accuracy is 1% higher once.” It is one or more of:

a consistent improvement across repeated seeds with confidence intervals;

improved external AUROC/AUPRC or sensitivity at fixed specificity;

better calibration;

lower degradation at reduced training-set sizes;

fewer trainable parameters with comparable performance;

stability under noise or source shift.

Negative result
A scientifically useful negative result would be:

“After matched embedding reduction and patient/source-separated evaluation, QSVM/VQC did not outperform RBF-SVM or DenseNet baselines; lung segmentation improved both classical and quantum pipelines similarly; quantum simulation/hardware cost remained higher.”

That is a legitimate and valuable contribution if well documented.

11. Internal SIH pitch
Narrative
Problem: TB remains a high-impact disease; CXR screening can be useful but models may learn spurious markers, vary by site, and fail to communicate uncertainty.

Existing AI: Classical deep learning can classify TB CXRs effectively, but deployment needs reliability, explainability, and external validation.

Research opportunity: QML in health is promising but lacks rigorous evidence of benefit, especially under real resource constraints and cross-dataset validation.

Hypothesis: Anatomically restricting the model to lung fields creates a more meaningful low-dimensional representation for hybrid quantum-classical learning.

Architecture: CXR → quality checks → lung segmentation → deep embedding → PCA → classical and quantum classifiers → calibrated risk + explanation + benchmark card.

Proof-of-work: Show both whole-image and segmented-lung branches; compare a classical baseline and QSVM using the same reduced feature space.

Credibility: State that quantum benefit is a hypothesis, not a conclusion. The platform is designed to test it fairly.

Future: Extend to TBX11K lesion localization, external validation, pneumonia, and hospital-ready validation only after clinical collaboration and regulatory assessment.
